Laser powder bed fusion links a localized, rapidly moving heat input to the solidification conditions that shape a manufactured material. Temperature gradients, cooling paths and repeated heating therefore matter when thermal models are used to interpret process–structure relationships, as well as when they are used to select process parameters. Resolving all the relevant interactions over practical build scales is expensive, which gives efficient heat-transfer models an important role. The question is how much thermal interpretation their accessible geometric comparisons can support. Melt-pool dimensions constrain a model, but the inferred temperature field also depends on how energy deposition, material response and experimental observation are represented. \cite{king2015,hooper2018,myers2023,review2024}

Moving-source conduction models offer a well-established route to efficient calculations. Analytical solutions and scaling relations expose the competition between heat input and diffusion, while nonlinear formulations add temperature-dependent properties and phase-change storage. These approaches have achieved useful geometry predictions under defined conditions. In the AMMT single-track benchmark, for example, a nonlinear temperature model with measured boundary input and effective directional conductivity reproduced melt-pool shapes closely. Such success establishes the value of simplified models, while retaining the need to assess their assumptions against the output of interest. Scaling-law comparisons also show that performance can depend on the material and processing regime. \cite{rosenthal1946,vanelsen2007,rubenchik2018,promoppatum2017,kollmannsberger2019,naderi2023}

Calibration and source design have addressed part of this problem. Inverse heat-conduction procedures can constrain effective source parameters from experiments and interpolate them across processing conditions. Bayesian adaptations incorporate material and powder effects, infer distributions of apparent inputs and propagate uncertainty in geometric predictions. Other studies compare volumetric source families or let source depth and absorption vary with local pool conditions. These strategies have improved agreement and extended the useful range of heat-transfer models within their tested windows. Their results also make source representation a necessary alternative to material closure when interpreting a shape discrepancy. \cite{ross2022,whalen2021,heatsourcebayes2024,dynamic2024}

Additional measurements clarify what geometric agreement leaves unresolved. Myers et al. showed that parameter combinations fitting geometry can produce different thermal profiles, and used measured temperature to distinguish between them. Independent energy-coupling studies further show that effective absorption varies with the surface and processing regime, while reflected light, calorimetric heat and model-fitted absorptivity do not represent identical quantities. Comparisons with thermal cameras introduce another layer: the temperature field must be related to the measured emission, including the effects of the surface and optical path. Geometry, energy and thermal data consequently provide complementary constraints. \cite{myers2023,trapp2017,simonds2018,lane2020absorption,ye2019,weeks2026}

Recent studies have examined this reliability from several directions. Material-parameter sensitivity has been quantified in analytical LPBF models, while comparisons with in-process images and post-process cross-sections reveal that correlation and agreement can differ across surface and depth observables. A fitting-free conduction diagnostic tests whether measured depths can be explained with physically feasible absorption and examines the robustness of that conclusion to effective diffusivity. These studies establish both the usefulness and the limits of reduced thermal descriptions. They leave room for a complementary, field-level diagnosis of what an explicitly chosen property continuation changes inside a fixed calibrated model. \cite{yang2024,soares2026,hong2026}

Here, a nonlinear moving-frame enthalpy model is used to examine that diagnosis for bare IN625 single tracks. The model retains an existing scalar calibration to one melt-pool length, and then evaluates shape comparisons and the response to separate and combined high-temperature continuations of thermal conductivity and sensible heat capacity. This choice addresses a concrete material-data limitation: the supplier tables end below the adopted solidus, so their continuation is part of the model closure. The analysis distinguishes movement of the rear solidus and liquidus positions from the interval between them, and distinguishes that spatial interval from the passage time derived using scan speed. These comparisons show which changes mainly move the tail, which alter its phase interval, and which arise from the mapping to material time. They provide a bounded interpretation of closure sensitivity after geometric calibration, while leaving independent temperature validation and physical selection of the high-temperature properties open. \cite{specialmetals625,lane2020}
